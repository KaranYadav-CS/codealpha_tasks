Module 01 ⏱️ 4 min read

What is Phishing & How Attacks Work

Phishing is a deceptive social engineering attack where malicious actors impersonate trusted entities to trick individuals into divulging sensitive data or executing unauthorized actions.

The 5 Stages of the Phishing Lifecycle:

  1. Reconnaissance: Harvesting names, email patterns, job titles, and vendor relationships from LinkedIn, company websites, and social media.
  2. Weaponization: Registering lookalike domains, setting up fraudulent credential harvesting landing pages, or crafting weaponized attachments.
  3. Delivery & Lure: Dispatching lures via mass spam, spear-phishing emails, SMS messages (smishing), or QR codes.
  4. Exploitation & Harvesting: Capturing login credentials, session cookies, multi-factor codes, or executing trojan payloads.
  5. Action on Objectives: Account takeover, exfiltration of corporate intellectual property, wire fraud, or ransomware deployment.
Core Rule: Phishing is never just a technical flaw; it is an orchestrated campaign exploiting trust, routine, and cognitive bias.
Module 02 ⏱️ 5 min read

Common Attack Vectors & Formats

Phishing attacks have expanded far beyond untargeted email spam into sophisticated multi-channel vectors targeting mobile devices and physical environments.

Primary Attack Classifications:

  • Email Phishing (Mass Spam vs Spear Phishing): Mass campaigns target thousands with generic lures, while spear-phishing targets specific employees with customized corporate context.
  • Whaling / CEO Fraud (BEC): Impersonating high-level executives or legal counsel to authorize emergency wire transfers or vendor account changes.
  • Smishing (SMS Phishing): Text messages claiming urgent delivery exceptions, toll violations, or bank fraud alerts, capitalizing on mobile screen limitations.
  • Vishing (Voice Phishing): Phone calls from fake IT Helpdesk representatives requesting password resets or remote desktop access.
  • Quishing (QR Code Phishing): Malicious QR codes printed on breakroom flyers or embedded as images in emails to bypass corporate text filters.
Core Rule: Attack vectors vary, but they all share a common mechanism: prompting you to visit an unverified portal or run an untrusted file.
Module 03 ⏱️ 6 min read

Anatomy of Warning Signs (Red Flags)

Deceptive messages leave discernible digital fingerprints. Knowing what to examine enables you to recognize an attack within seconds.

Key Indicators to Scrutinize:

  • Display Name Spoofing: The friendly name reads "Corporate IT Support", but the real email address is on an unrecognized external domain.
  • Lookalike Domains (Typosquatting): Substituting letters (micros0ft.com, paypa1.com) or adding misleading prefixes (chase.com.verification-auth.invalid).
  • Hyperlink Deception: Anchor text displays a legitimate URL (https://mybank.com), but hovering reveals an external destination.
  • Suspicious Attachments: Macro-enabled Office files (.xlsm, .docm), disguised archives (.iso, .vhd), or double extensions (Invoice.pdf.exe).
  • Generic Salutations & Errors: Greetings like "Dear Customer" or "Dear Employee" combined with awkward phrasing or artificial urgency.
Core Rule: Always check the actual domain after the @ in the sender address and hover over links to inspect the true destination.
Module 04 ⏱️ 4 min read

Social Engineering & Psychological Triggers

Attackers weaponize Robert Cialdini's principles of persuasion to create emotional states that bypass logical scrutiny and analytical thinking.

The 6 Core Cognitive Exploits:

  • Manufactured Urgency: Countdowns and deadlines ("Account terminates in 2 hours") force impulsive actions.
  • Appeals to Authority: Impersonating CEOs, law enforcement, tax agencies, or corporate IT executives to exploit deference.
  • Fear & Intimidation: Threatening lawsuits, termination, or credit ruin to provoke anxiety.
  • Greed & Curiosity: Offering unexpected bonuses, tax rebates, gift cards, or confidential payroll leaks.
  • Helpfulness & Sympathy: Posing as an overburdened colleague or stranded executive in distress.
Core Rule: Whenever a message sparks strong emotion—fear, panic, excitement, or urgency—hit the mental pause button and verify.
Module 05 ⏱️ 5 min read

Technical Verification Protocols

Relying on intuition is insufficient. Implementing structured technical validation techniques ensures you never get fooled by spoofed communications.

Essential Verification Methods:

  • The HTTPS Padlock Fallacy: HTTPS only encrypts communication; it does not validate ownership. Over 80% of active phishing websites now use valid SSL/TLS certificates.
  • Email Authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC): SPF authorizes IP senders, DKIM validates cryptographic signatures, and DMARC specifies enforcement. However, they do not stop newly registered lookalike domains.
  • URL Parsing Discipline: Identify the Root Domain. In login.example.com.phish.test, the true domain is phish.test, not example.com.
  • Out-of-Band (OOB) Verification: Contact the sender via an independent, pre-established phone number or in-person channel—never using contact details inside the suspect message.
Core Rule: If an email requests financial transfers, password resets, or sensitive data, mandatory Out-of-Band confirmation is non-negotiable.
Module 06 ⏱️ 5 min read

Incident Response & Remediation

What you do in the first five minutes after suspecting a compromise can mean the difference between an isolated error and an enterprise-wide catastrophe.

Immediate Incident Playbook:

  1. If you only received a suspicious message: Do not click, forward original message with full headers (.eml) to your IT/SOC team, and delete.
  2. If you clicked a link or entered credentials:
    • Immediately change the compromised password from a separate, clean device.
    • Disconnect the affected workstation from Wi-Fi and Ethernet (do NOT power off, to preserve RAM evidence).
    • Notify IT Security immediately so they can revoke active session tokens and check audit logs.
    • If financial details were entered, immediately contact your banking institution to freeze cards/accounts.
Core Rule: Never conceal an accidental click. Prompt reporting allows security defenders to remediate before adversaries exploit access.

Emergency Action Protocol

Post-Incident Response Runbook

Follow this structured incident response sequence if you suspect you or a colleague interacted with a phishing lure.

Step 1

Isolate & Disconnect

Disconnect the affected device from Wi-Fi and unplug the Ethernet cable immediately. This halts lateral network traversal, C2 beaconing, and automated data exfiltration.

Rule: Do NOT power off or reboot the system; doing so destroys volatile RAM forensic memory.
Step 2

Rotate Credentials

From a completely separate, trusted, uncompromised device, immediately change the password for the affected account. Change any identical passwords on other services.

Rule: Utilize a strong, unique 16+ character passphrase or password manager.
Step 3

Revoke Active Sessions

Log into your account security settings and select "Sign out of all sessions" to invalidate active OAuth tokens and session cookies hijacked by attackers.

Rule: Re-verify your registered Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) devices and phone numbers.
Step 4

Notify Security / IT

Contact your organization's IT Helpdesk or SOC immediately. Forward the raw phishing message as an attachment (.eml) so headers and payloads can be analyzed.

Rule: Never feel ashamed or delay reporting; early notification saves organizations millions.
Step 5

Report & Protect

If personal financial or identity information was shared, immediately contact your bank to freeze cards and place a fraud alert on credit bureaus (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion).

Rule: File an official complaint with FTC ReportFraud and CISA.
